Cat research for students and researchers

By the MeowWise team · Published 8 October 2026 · Last reviewed: 28 October 2026

The peer reviewed papers behind MeowWise, with full citations and DOIs. We opened every paper listed here and checked its details against the publisher or PubMed record before adding it. Each entry says in one or two sentences what the paper found and links to the MeowWise pages that use it. Papers marked free full text can be read at no cost on PubMed Central or the publisher's site.

How to use this list

  • Students: start with the summary, then read the paper's abstract through the DOI link. Abstracts are free even when the full paper is not. Ask your school or public librarian if you need a paywalled paper.
  • Teachers: several papers anchor lessons on our teachers hub, such as the sweet taste gene (Li 2005) for HS-LS3-1 and Scottish Fold genetics (Gandolfi 2016) for MS-LS4-5.
  • Citing MeowWise: every article ends with a Cite this page box in APA, MLA and Chicago style. For research work, cite the original papers below.

Domestication, history and breeds

  1. Driscoll C.A., Menotti-Raymond M., Roca A.L., Hupe K., Johnson W.E., Geffen E., Harley E.H., Delibes M., Pontier D., Kitchener A.C., Yamaguchi N., O'Brien S.J. and Macdonald D.W. (2007). The Near Eastern origin of cat domestication. Science, 317(5837), 519 to 523. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1139518. Free full text (PMC).
    A genetic study of 979 domestic cats and wildcats concluded that cats were domesticated in the Near East, probably alongside early farming villages, from at least five founders.
    Used on: Cat fact sheet, Cat science quiz.
  2. Vigne J.D., Guilaine J., Debue K., Haye L. and Gérard P. (2004). Early taming of the cat in Cyprus. Science, 304(5668), 259. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1095335.
    Describes a cat buried close to a human grave at the Neolithic village of Shillourokambos, Cyprus, about 9,500 years ago. The authors call it taming rather than domestication, because the cat's skeleton shows no domestic changes.
    Used on: Cat fact sheet, Cat science quiz.
  3. Ottoni C., Van Neer W., De Cupere B., et al. (2017). The palaeogenetics of cat dispersal in the ancient world. Nature Ecology and Evolution, 1, 0139. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41559-017-0139.
    Ancient DNA from archaeological cat remains shows that Near Eastern and Egyptian wildcat populations both contributed to domestic cats at different times, with spread along human trade routes. A blotched tabby coat variant became common only after the Middle Ages.
    Used on: Cat science quiz.
  4. Lipinski M.J., Froenicke L., Baysac K.C., et al. (2008). The ascent of cat breeds: genetic evaluations of breeds and worldwide random-bred populations. Genomics, 91(1), 12 to 21. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ygeno.2007.10.009. Free full text (PMC).
    Genetic data from over 1,100 cats in 22 breeds and 17 random bred populations on five continents. Most breeds came from local cats of their claimed region of origin.
    Used on: Cat traits worksheet, Teacher lesson ideas.
  5. Montague M.J., Li G., Gandolfi B., et al. (2014). Comparative analysis of the domestic cat genome reveals genetic signatures underlying feline biology and domestication. Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences of the USA, 111(48), 17230 to 17235. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1410083111. Free full text (PMC).
    A high quality cat reference genome. Found signs of selection in genes for fat metabolism (suited to a meat diet) and for vision and hearing, and an expanded pheromone detection system at the expense of odor detection.
    Used on: Cat adaptations lesson plan, Cat science quiz.
  6. Clark D.L. and Clark R.A. (2016). Neutral point testing of color vision in the domestic cat. Experimental Eye Research, 153, 23 to 26. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.exer.2016.10.002.
    Two cats discriminated monochromatic LEDs from white across many wavelengths but failed at 505 nm, supporting dichromatic vision with a neutral point near that of a human deuteranope.
    Used on: Can cats see color?, Myths vs facts.
  7. Daw N.W. and Pearlman A.L. (1970). Cat colour vision: evidence for more than one cone process. The Journal of Physiology, 211(1), 125 to 137. https://doi.org/10.1113/jphysiol.1970.sp009270. Free full text (PMC).
    Cats kept discriminating red or orange from cyan after rods were saturated; rare LGN opponent cells combined green absorbing and blue absorbing cone input.
    Used on: Can cats see color?.
  8. Loop M.S., Millican C.L. and Thomas S.R. (1987). Photopic spectral sensitivity of the cat. The Journal of Physiology, 382, 537 to 553. https://doi.org/10.1113/jphysiol.1987.sp016383.
    On photopic white backgrounds, spectral sensitivity peaked near about 454 nm and 561 nm, consistent with independent blue and green cone action and no separate 500 nm photopic mechanism.
    Used on: Can cats see color?.
  9. Linberg K.A., Lewis G.P., Shaaw C., Rex T.S. and Fisher S.K. (2001). Distribution of S- and M-cones in normal and experimentally detached cat retina. Journal of Comparative Neurology, 430(3), 343 to 356. https://doi.org/10.1002/1096-9861(20010212)430:3<343::AID-CNE1035>3.0.CO;2-U.
    M-cones made up about 80% to 90% of cones (peak over 26,000 per mm2 at the area centralis); S-cones about 10% to 20%, confirming two spectral cone classes across the retina.
    Used on: Can cats see color?.
  10. Ollivier F.J., Samuelson D.A., Brooks D.E., Lewis P.A., Kallberg M.E. and Komaromy A.M. (2004). Comparative morphology of the tapetum lucidum (among selected species). Veterinary Ophthalmology, 7(1), 11 to 22. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1463-5224.2004.00318.x.
    Review classifying tapeta by location and composition; carnivores, including cats, have a choroidal tapetum cellulosum, which reflects light back through the photoreceptors to boost sensitivity in dim light.
    Used on: Why do cats' eyes glow in the dark?.
  11. Schwab I.R., Yuen C.K., Buyukmihci N.C., Blankenship T.N. and Fitzgerald P.G. (2002). Evolution of the tapetum. Transactions of the American Ophthalmological Society, 100, 187 to 200. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1358962/.
    Comparative histology suggesting tapeta evolved independently at least three times, possibly in the Devonian, with each reflecting the wavelengths most relevant to the species' niche.
    Used on: Why do cats' eyes glow in the dark?.
  12. Banks M.S., Sprague W.W., Schmoll J., Parnell J.A.Q. and Love G.D. (2015). Why do animal eyes have pupils of different shapes? Science Advances, 1(7), e1500391. https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.1500391.
    Across 214 land species, vertical slit pupils were linked to ambush predators active day and night; the domestic cat pupil changes area about 135 fold versus about 15 fold in humans.
    Used on: Why do cats' eyes glow in the dark?.
  13. Malmstrom T. and Kroger R.H.H. (2006). Pupil shapes and lens optics in the eyes of terrestrial vertebrates. Journal of Experimental Biology, 209(1), 18 to 25. https://doi.org/10.1242/jeb.01959.
    Slit pupils occurred only in animals with multifocal lenses; small felids have slit pupils and multifocal lenses, large felids round pupils and monofocal lenses.
    Used on: Why do cats' eyes glow in the dark?.

Behavior and cognition

  1. Cecchetti M., Crowley S.L., Goodwin C.E.D. and McDonald R.A. (2021). Provision of high meat content food and object play reduce predation of wild animals by domestic cats Felis catus. Current Biology, 31(5), 1107 to 1111.e5. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2020.12.044.
    Controlled household trial: high meat protein, grain free food cut prey brought home by 36% and daily object play by 25%; puzzle feeders raised it by 33%; Birdsbesafe collar covers cut birds by 42%; bells had no discernible effect.
    Used in how do I stop my cat hunting?
  2. Ruxton G.D., Thomas S. and Wright J.W. (2002). Bells reduce predation of wildlife by domestic cats (Felis catus). Journal of Zoology, 256(1), 81 to 83. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0952836902000109.
    21 owners in Lancashire: cats delivered a mean of 2.9 prey in 4 belled weeks versus 5.5 in 4 weeks without a bell.
    Used in how do I stop my cat hunting?
  3. Lockwood H.L., Bulling M. and Huck M. (2025). What the cat dragged in: quantifying prey return rates of pet cats (Felis catus) with outdoor access in the UK. Ecology and Evolution, 15(3), e71063. https://doi.org/10.1002/ece3.71063. Free full text (PMC).
    553 UK cats over up to 43 months returned 7,013 prey (mean 1.5 per cat per month, median zero; 83% mammals); a few super predators returned over 15 a month.
    Used in how do I stop my cat hunting?
  4. Loss S.R., Will T. and Marra P.P. (2013). The impact of free-ranging domestic cats on wildlife of the United States. Nature Communications, 4, 1396. https://doi.org/10.1038/ncomms2380.
    Systematic review estimating free ranging cats kill 1.4 to 3.7 billion birds and 6.9 to 20.7 billion mammals a year in the US, mostly by unowned cats.
    Used in how do I stop my cat hunting?
  5. Wells D.L. and Millsopp S. (2009). Lateralized behaviour in the domestic cat, Felis silvestris catus. Animal Behaviour, 78(2), 537 to 541. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.anbehav.2009.06.010.
    In 42 pet cats, a complex treat retrieval task revealed a consistent paw preference in all but one cat; females tended to use the right paw and males the left.
    Used in are cats left or right pawed?
  6. Wells D.L. and Millsopp S. (2012). The ontogenesis of lateralized behavior in the domestic cat, Felis silvestris catus. Journal of Comparative Psychology, 126(1), 23 to 30. https://doi.org/10.1037/a0026522.
    Kittens were more often ambilateral at 12 weeks and 6 months but more often paw preferent by 1 year, with the same sex split.
    Used in are cats left or right pawed?
  7. McDowell L.J., Wells D.L. and Hepper P.G. (2018). Lateralization of spontaneous behaviours in the domestic cat, Felis silvestris. Animal Behaviour, 135, 37 to 43. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.anbehav.2017.11.002.
    In 44 pet cats, most showed a side bias for food reaching (73 percent), stepping down stairs (70 percent) and stepping over objects (66 percent), but not for lying side.
    Used in are cats left or right pawed?
  8. Hart B.L., Hart L.A., Thigpen A.P. and Willits N.H. (2021). Characteristics of plant eating in domestic cats. Animals, 11(7), 1853. https://doi.org/10.3390/ani11071853. Free full text (PMC).
    Two owner surveys found plant eating is common (65 percent of cats weekly in one survey), rarely preceded by signs of illness, and no more frequent in long haired cats, arguing against the illness and hairball explanations.
    Used in why do cats eat grass?
  9. Franck A.R. and Farid A. (2020). Many species of the Carnivora consume grass and other fibrous plant tissues. Belgian Journal of Zoology, 150, 1 to 70. https://doi.org/10.26496/bjz.2020.73.
    A review of 352 diet studies found fibrous plant eating in 124 carnivore species and grass in 95, with nine studies noting parasites alongside grass in scats.
    Used in why do cats eat grass?
  10. Cannon M. (2013). Hair balls in cats: a normal nuisance or a sign that something is wrong? Journal of Feline Medicine and Surgery, 15(1), 21 to 29. https://doi.org/10.1177/1098612X12470342.
    A clinical review arguing that frequent hairballs in most short haired cats point to an underlying problem such as itchy skin, fleas or a gut disorder.
    Used in why do cats eat grass?
  11. Vinke C.M., Godijn L.M. and van der Leij W.J.R. (2014). Will a hiding box provide stress reduction for shelter cats? Applied Animal Behaviour Science, 160, 86 to 93. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.applanim.2014.09.002.
    19 newly arrived shelter cats were randomly given a hiding box (10) or none (9); the box group had significantly lower Cat Stress Scores on days 3 and 4 and reached by about day 3 the level both groups shared at day 14.
    Used on: Why do cats like boxes?, Cat behavior.
  12. Kry K. and Casey R. (2007). The effect of hiding enrichment on stress levels and behaviour of domestic cats (Felis sylvestris catus) in a shelter setting and the implications for adoption potential. Animal Welfare, 16(3), 375 to 383. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0962728600027196.
    In 43 kennelled cats, a hide box lowered stress across the study days, increased approach and relaxed behavior, and made no significant difference to days until adoption or adoption rate.
    Used on: Why do cats like boxes?.
  13. Smith G.E., Chouinard P.A. and Byosiere S.E. (2021). If I fits I sits: A citizen science investigation into illusory contour susceptibility in domestic cats (Felis silvestris catus). Applied Animal Behaviour Science, 240, 105338. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.applanim.2021.105338.
    In home trials, the nine cats that chose a shape sat in a real square 8 times, an illusory Kanizsa square 7 times and a control once, suggesting cats treat illusory contours like real ones (small sample, high dropout).
    Used on: Why do cats like boxes?.
  14. Uenoyama R., Miyazaki T., Hurst J.L., Beynon R.J., Adachi M., Murooka T., Onoda I., Miyazawa Y., Katayama R., Yamashita T., Kaneko S., Nishikawa T. and Miyazaki M. (2021). The characteristic response of domestic cats to plant iridoids allows them to gain chemical defense against mosquitoes. Science Advances, 7(4), eabd9135. https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.abd9135. Free full text (PMC).
    Identified nepetalactol as silver vine's main active iridoid, showed it raises plasma beta endorphin and that blocking mu opioid receptors suppresses rubbing, and found that rubbing transfers it to the face and head where it repels Aedes albopictus mosquitoes.
    Used on: Why do cats like catnip?, Cat behavior.
  15. Uenoyama R., Miyazaki T., Adachi M., Nishikawa T., Hurst J.L. and Miyazaki M. (2022). Domestic cat damage to plant leaves containing iridoids enhances chemical repellency to pests. iScience, 25(7), 104455. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.isci.2022.104455. Free full text (PMC).
    Licking and chewing catnip and silver vine sharply increased iridoid emission, and the more varied cocktail from damaged silver vine repelled mosquitoes at low concentration.
    Used on: Why do cats like catnip?.
  16. Bol S., Caspers J., Buckingham L., Anderson-Shelton G.D., Ridgway C., Buffington C.A.T., Schulz S. and Bunnik E.M. (2017). Responsiveness of cats (Felidae) to silver vine (Actinidia polygama), Tatarian honeysuckle (Lonicera tatarica), valerian (Valeriana officinalis) and catnip (Nepeta cataria). BMC Veterinary Research, 13, 70. https://doi.org/10.1186/s12917-017-0987-6. Free full text (PMC).
    Of 100 domestic cats, about one in three ignored catnip while almost 80% responded to silver vine; nearly 75% of catnip non responders responded to silver vine.
    Used on: Why do cats like catnip?, Cat behavior.
  17. Nicastro N. (2004). Perceptual and acoustic evidence for species-level differences in meow vocalizations by domestic cats (Felis catus) and African wild cats (Felis silvestris lybica). Journal of Comparative Psychology, 118(3), 287 to 296. https://doi.org/10.1037/0735-7036.118.3.287.
    Domestic meows were shorter and had higher mean formant and fundamental frequencies than African wild cat cries; human listeners rated domestic meows as far more pleasant, consistent with selection under human perceptual biases.
    Used on: Why do cats meow at humans?, Cat behavior.
  18. Nicastro N. and Owren M.J. (2003). Classification of domestic cat (Felis catus) vocalizations by naive and experienced human listeners. Journal of Comparative Psychology, 117(1), 44 to 52. https://doi.org/10.1037/0735-7036.117.1.44.
    Listeners classified meows from 12 cats in five contexts above chance but only modestly; bouts beat single calls, and cat experienced people did better. Meows act as nonspecific attention getters more than fixed referential words.
    Used on: Why do cats meow at humans?.
  19. Yeon S.C., Kim Y.K., Park S.J., Lee S.S., Lee S.Y., Suh E.H., Houpt K.A., Chang H.H., Lee H.C., Yang B.G. and Lee H.J. (2011). Differences between vocalization evoked by social stimuli in feral cats and house cats. Behavioural Processes, 87(2), 183 to 189. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.beproc.2011.03.003.
    Feral (n = 25) versus house (n = 13) cats across five social tests: feral cats called more and showed more fear aggression; house cat meows had higher frequency measures and shorter duration than feral meows in agonistic contexts.
    Used on: Why do cats meow at humans?.
  20. Tavernier C., Ahmed S., Houpt K.A. and Yeon S.C. (2020). Feline vocal communication. Journal of Veterinary Science, 21(1), e18. https://doi.org/10.4142/jvs.2020.21.e18. Free full text (open access).
    Review of the domestic cat vocal repertoire: the meow is rare in adult cat to cat talk and common toward humans as an attention call shaped by domestication and socialization.
    Used on: Why do cats meow at humans?.
  21. McComb K., Taylor A.M., Wilson C. and Charlton B.D. (2009). The cry embedded within the purr. Current Biology, 19(13), R507 to R508. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2009.05.033.
    People (n = 50) rated food solicitation purrs from ten cats as more urgent and less pleasant; a voiced peak (220 to 520 Hz, mean 380 Hz) above the roughly 27 Hz purr drove the urgency, and removing it made purrs sound less urgent.
    Used on: Why do cats purr?, Cat behavior.
  22. Herbst C.T., Prigge T., Garcia M., Hampala V., Hofer R., Weissengruber G.E., Svec J.G. and Fitch W.T. (2023). Domestic cat larynges can produce purring frequencies without neural input. Current Biology, 33(21), 4727 to 4732.e4. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2023.09.014.
    All eight excised cat larynges oscillated at purring rates (25 to 30 Hz) with no neural input; connective tissue masses up to 4 mm in the vocal folds may allow the very low pitch.
    Used on: Why do cats purr?.
  23. Humphrey T., Proops L., Forman J., Spooner R. and McComb K. (2020). The role of cat eye narrowing movements in cat-human communication. Scientific Reports, 10, 16503. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-020-73426-0. Free full text (PMC).
    Two experiments in cats' homes: cats narrowed their eyes more when owners slow blinked at them, and were more likely to approach an unfamiliar experimenter who slow blinked.
    Used on: Why do cats slow blink?, Cat behavior, Myths vs facts.
  24. Vitale K.R., Behnke A.C. and Udell M.A.R. (2019). Attachment bonds between domestic cats and humans. Current Biology, 29(18), R864 to R865. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2019.08.036.
    Secure base test: 64.3% of 70 classifiable kittens and 65.8% of 38 adult cats were securely attached to their owner; a six week training and socialization class did not change kittens' attachment styles.
    Used on: Do cats love their owners?, Cat behavior.
  25. Potter A. and Mills D.S. (2015). Domestic cats (Felis silvestris catus) do not show signs of secure attachment to their owners. PLOS ONE, 10(9), e0135109. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0135109. Free full text (open access).
    Counterbalanced strange situation test with 20 cat and owner pairs: cats meowed more when the owner left, but showed no other sign of using the owner as a secure base.
    Used on: Do cats love their owners?.
  26. Vitale Shreve K.R., Mehrkam L.R. and Udell M.A.R. (2017). Social interaction, food, scent or toys? A formal assessment of domestic pet and shelter cat (Felis silvestris catus) preferences. Behavioural Processes, 141, 322 to 328. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.beproc.2017.03.016.
    Free operant preference test: social interaction with humans was the most preferred category for most pet and shelter cats, followed by food.
    Used on: Do cats love their owners?.
  27. Saito A., Shinozuka K., Ito Y. and Hasegawa T. (2019). Domestic cats (Felis catus) discriminate their names from other words. Scientific Reports, 9, 5394. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-019-40616-4. Free full text (open access).
    Four habituation experiments: household cats reacted more to their own name than to matched everyday nouns or housemates' names, including when strangers spoke; cat café cats did not separate their name from housemates' names.
    Used on: Do cats know their names?, Cat behavior.
  28. Takagi S., Saito A., Arahori M., Chijiiwa H., Koyasu H., Nagasawa M., Kikusui T., Fujita K. and Kuroshima H. (2022). Cats learn the names of their friend cats in their daily lives. Scientific Reports, 12, 6155. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-022-10261-5. Free full text (open access).
    Household cats looked longer at a housemate's photo when it did not match the name they had just heard, suggesting they link cat names to faces without training; café cats did not.
    Used on: Do cats know their names?.
  29. Saito A. and Shinozuka K. (2013). Vocal recognition of owners by domestic cats (Felis catus). Animal Cognition, 16(4), 685 to 690. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10071-013-0620-4.
    With 20 cats and a habituation test, cats responded less and less to strangers calling their name and then responded again to their owner's voice, mostly with ear and head movements. Cats can tell people apart by voice alone.
    Used on: Do cats know their names?, Cat fact sheet, Cat adaptations lesson plan.
  30. Vitale K.R., Behnke A.C. and Udell M.A.R. (2019). Attachment bonds between domestic cats and humans. Current Biology, 29(18), R864 to R865. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2019.08.036.
    Using behavioral criteria from human infant research, the authors report that cats show distinct attachment styles toward their human caregivers.

Senses and nutrition

  1. Heffner R.S. and Heffner H.E. (1985). Hearing range of the domestic cat. Hearing Research, 19(1), 85 to 88. https://doi.org/10.1016/0378-5955(85)90100-5.
    Behavioral hearing tests in two cats found a range of 48 Hz to 85 kHz for sounds at 70 dB SPL, one of the broadest hearing ranges among mammals.
    Used on: Cat adaptations lesson plan, Cat science quiz.
  2. Lei W., Ravoninjohary A., Li X., Margolskee R.F., Reed D.R., Beauchamp G.K. and Jiang P. (2015). Functional analyses of bitter taste receptors in domestic cats (Felis catus). PLOS ONE, 10(10), e0139670. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0139670. Free full text (open access).
    Cell based assays found at least seven functional cat bitter taste receptors with distinct ranges; other land living carnivores had roughly similar numbers, so a meat diet did not clearly reduce bitter taste.
    Used on: Can cats taste sweet?.
  3. Li X., Li W., Wang H., Cao J., Maehashi K., Huang L., Bachmanov A.A., Reed D.R., Legrand-Defretin V., Beauchamp G.K. and Brand J.G. (2005). Pseudogenization of a sweet-receptor gene accounts for cats' indifference toward sugar. PLoS Genetics, 1(1), 27 to 35. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pgen.0010003. Free full text (PMC).
    The cat Tas1r2 gene carries a 247 base pair deletion and stop codons and is not expressed, so cats lack a working sweet taste receptor. Tigers and cheetahs share the change.
    Used on: Can cats taste sweet?, Cat nutrition, Cat fact sheet, Cat adaptations lesson plan.
  4. Li X., Li W., Wang H., Bayley D.L., Cao J., Reed D.R., Bachmanov A.A., Huang L., Legrand-Defretin V., Beauchamp G.K. and Brand J.G. (2006). Cats lack a sweet taste receptor. Journal of Nutrition, 136(7), 1932S to 1934S. https://doi.org/10.1093/jn/136.7.1932S. Free full text (PMC).
    Summary of the cat sweet receptor work with a comparison to dogs: cats neither prefer nor avoid sugars, their taste nerves do not respond to sucrose, and cat Tas1r2 is an unexpressed pseudogene while dog Tas1r2 is intact.
    Used on: Can cats taste sweet?.
  5. McGrane S.J., Gibbs M., Hernangomez de Alvaro C., Dunlop N., Winnig M., Klebansky B. and Waller D. (2023). Umami taste perception and preferences of the domestic cat (Felis catus), an obligate carnivore. Chemical Senses, 48, bjad026. https://doi.org/10.1093/chemse/bjad026. Free full text (PMC).
    Cats express Tas1r1 in taste papillae; their umami receptor is activated by nucleotides (purines strongest) and enhanced by 11 L amino acids, but not by glutamic or aspartic acid. Umami is proposed as the cat's main appetitive taste.
    Used on: Can cats taste sweet?, Cat nutrition.
  6. Pion P.D., Kittleson M.D., Rogers Q.R. and Morris J.G. (1987). Myocardial failure in cats associated with low plasma taurine: a reversible cardiomyopathy. Science, 237(4816), 764 to 768. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.3616607.
    Low blood taurine was linked to heart muscle failure in cats, and taurine supplements were associated with normal heart function returning.
    Used on: Cat nutrition, Cat fact sheet.
  7. Sadek T., Hamper B., Horwitz D., Rodan I., Rowe E. and Sundahl E. (2018). Feline feeding programs: addressing behavioral needs to improve feline health and wellbeing. Journal of Feline Medicine and Surgery, 20(11), 1049 to 1055. https://doi.org/10.1177/1098612X18791877. Free full text (PMC).
    AAFP consensus statement on feeding in a way that fits natural cat behavior: frequent small meals, puzzle feeders, foraging and separate food and water stations.
    Used on: Cat nutrition, Cat care basics.

Locomotion and reflexes

  1. Kane T.R. and Scher M.P. (1969). A dynamical explanation of the falling cat phenomenon. International Journal of Solids and Structures, 5(7), 663 to 670. https://doi.org/10.1016/0020-7683(69)90086-9.
    Two body model of a falling cat: the torso bends but does not twist; a forward, side, back, other side, forward bend cycle reorients the body with dynamics that match filmed air righting and conserve angular momentum.
    Used on: How do cats land on their feet?, Myths vs facts.
  2. Arabyan A. and Tsai D. (1998). A distributed control model for the air-righting reflex of a cat. Biological Cybernetics, 79(5), 393 to 401. https://doi.org/10.1007/s004220050488.
    Multisegment, multijoint simulation of zero momentum self righting with a two stage decentralized controller for a fast turn then a gravity aligned hold; many joints allow multiple solutions for the same task.
    Used on: How do cats land on their feet?.
  3. Cremieux J., Veraart C. and Wanet M.C. (1984). Development of the air righting reflex in cats visually deprived since birth. Experimental Brain Research, 54(3), 564 to 566. https://doi.org/10.1007/BF00235481.
    Kittens blinded from birth matured air righting on the same schedule as sighted kittens (by 33 days), supporting primarily vestibular control of the reflex.
    Used on: How do cats land on their feet?, Myths vs facts.
  4. Windle W.F. and Fish M.W. (1932). The development of the vestibular righting reflex in the cat. Journal of Comparative Neurology, 54(1), 85 to 96. https://doi.org/10.1002/cne.900540107.
    Early developmental study linking postural righting in cats to maturation of the vestibular system and related neural pathways.
    Used on: How do cats land on their feet?.

Genetics and health

  1. Kaelin C.B., McGowan K.A., Trotman J.C., et al. and Barsh G.S. (2025). Molecular and genetic characterization of sex-linked orange coat color in the domestic cat. Current Biology, 35(12), 2826 to 2836.e5. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2025.04.055. Free full text (PMC).
    A roughly 5 kb deletion that switches on Arhgap36 in hair follicle pigment cells causes Sex linked orange; it lowers protein kinase A and matched orange coat color in 188 additional cats.
    Used in why are orange cats usually male?
  2. Toh H., Au Yeung W.K., Unoki M., et al. and Sasaki H. (2025). A deletion at the X-linked ARHGAP36 gene locus is associated with the orange coloration of tortoiseshell and calico cats. Current Biology, 35(12), 2816 to 2825.e3. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2025.03.075.
    A 5.1 kb intronic deletion in ARHGAP36 is closely and exclusively associated with orange coloration and appears to have a single origin.
    Used in why are orange cats usually male?
  3. Szczerbal I., Nowacka-Woszuk J. and Switonski M. (2026). XX/XY chimerism in tortoiseshell tomcats: a new case and review of the literature. Sexual Development, 20(1 to 6), 68 to 74. https://doi.org/10.1159/000551472.
    A fertile tortoiseshell Maine Coon tomcat carried both XX and XY cell lines; tricolored males usually have an unusual sex chromosome constitution.
    Used in why are orange cats usually male?
  4. O'Neill D.G., Church D.B., McGreevy P.D., Thomson P.C. and Brodbelt D.C. (2015). Longevity and mortality of cats attending primary care veterinary practices in England. Journal of Feline Medicine and Surgery, 17(2), 125 to 133. https://doi.org/10.1177/1098612X14536176. Free full text (PMC).
    VetCompass records of 4,009 cats that died: median longevity 14.0 years (IQR 9.0 to 17.0); crossbreds outlived purebreds (14.0 vs 12.5 years); trauma and kidney disorders were the most common recorded causes of death.
    Used on: How long do cats live?, Senior cat care.
  5. Teng K.T., Brodbelt D.C., Church D.B. and O'Neill D.G. (2024). Life tables of annual life expectancy and risk factors for mortality in cats in the UK. Journal of Feline Medicine and Surgery, 26(5), 1098612X241234556. https://doi.org/10.1177/1098612X241234556. Free full text (PMC).
    First UK cat life tables from 7,936 deaths in 2019: life expectancy at birth 11.74 years (females 12.51, males 11.18); Burmese and Birman longest, Sphynx shortest; entire, purebred and non ideal weight cats lived shorter lives.
    Used on: How long do cats live?, Senior cat care, Breeds.
  6. Montoya M., Morrison J.A., Arrignon F., Spofford N., Charles H., Hours M.A. and Biourge V. (2023). Life expectancy tables for dogs and cats derived from clinical data. Frontiers in Veterinary Science, 10, 1082102. https://doi.org/10.3389/fvets.2023.1082102. Free full text (PMC).
    Banfield Pet Hospital records of 2,390,078 US cats (2013 to 2019): life expectancy at birth 11.18 years; females 11.68 vs males 10.72 years.
    Used on: How long do cats live?.
  7. Gandolfi B., Alamri S., Darby W.G., et al. (2016). A dominant TRPV4 variant underlies osteochondrodysplasia in Scottish fold cats. Osteoarthritis and Cartilage, 24(8), 1441 to 1450. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joca.2016.03.019.
    A genome wide study of 44 Scottish Fold and 54 control cats found that the breed's dominantly inherited folded ears and bone and cartilage disease are associated with a variant in the TRPV4 gene, absent in 648 unaffected cats.
    Used on: Cat science quiz, Cat traits worksheet.
  8. Longeri M., Ferrari P., Knafelz P., et al. (2013). Myosin-binding protein C DNA variants in domestic cats (A31P, A74T, R820W) and their association with hypertrophic cardiomyopathy. Journal of Veterinary Internal Medicine, 27(2), 275 to 285. https://doi.org/10.1111/jvim.12031. Free full text (PMC).
    Genotyped 1,855 cats from 28 breeds and random bred cats worldwide for MYBPC3 variants, comparing results with heart scans in 446 cats.
    Used on: Cat breeds, Cat science quiz.
  9. Taylor S., Boysen S., Buffington T., Chalhoub S., Defauw P., Delgado M.M., Gunn-Moore D. and Korman R. (2025). 2025 iCatCare consensus guidelines on the diagnosis and management of lower urinary tract diseases in cats. Journal of Feline Medicine and Surgery, 27(2), 1098612X241309176. https://doi.org/10.1177/1098612X241309176. Free full text (PMC).
    Expert consensus on diagnosing and treating lower urinary tract diseases in cats, including urethral obstruction.
    Used on: Cat straining to pee.

Veterinary care guidelines (peer reviewed consensus)

  1. Ellis S.L.H., Rodan I., Carney H.C., Heath S., Rochlitz I., Shearburn L.D., Sundahl E. and Westropp J.L. (2013). AAFP and ISFM feline environmental needs guidelines. Journal of Feline Medicine and Surgery, 15(3), 219 to 230. https://doi.org/10.1177/1098612X13477537. Free full text (PMC).
    Treats meeting a cat's environmental needs as essential, not optional, and organizes them around five pillars meant to reduce stress, stress related disease and unwanted behavior.
    Used on: Cat care basics, Cat behavior, How many litter boxes?, Why do cats like boxes?.
  2. Carney H.C., Sadek T.P., Curtis T.M., et al. (2014). AAFP and ISFM guidelines for diagnosing and solving house-soiling behavior in cats. Journal of Feline Medicine and Surgery, 16(7), 579 to 598. https://doi.org/10.1177/1098612X14539092. Free full text (PMC).
    Practical guidance for preventing and solving house soiling, including litter box number, size and placement.
    Used on: How many litter boxes?, Cat behavior, Cat care basics.
  3. Quimby J., Gowland S., Carney H.C., DePorter T., Plummer P. and Westropp J. (2021). 2021 AAHA/AAFP Feline Life Stage Guidelines. Journal of Feline Medicine and Surgery, 23(3), 211 to 233. https://doi.org/10.1177/1098612X21993657. Free full text (PMC).
    Divides a cat's life into kitten, young adult, mature adult and senior stages plus end of life, with care recommendations for each.
    Used on: How often should a cat see the vet?.
  4. Ray M., Carney H.C., Boynton B., Quimby J., Robertson S., St Denis K., Tuzio H. and Wright B. (2021). 2021 AAFP Feline Senior Care Guidelines. Journal of Feline Medicine and Surgery, 23(7), 613 to 638. https://doi.org/10.1177/1098612X211021538. Free full text (PMC).
    Guidance on assessing and caring for older cats, introducing frailty as part of the senior cat assessment.
    Used on: Senior cats.
