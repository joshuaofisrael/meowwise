Most cats prefer to be petted on the head: the cheeks, the base of the ears and, for some cats, under the chin. The area cats tend to like least is the base of the tail. Belly, back, flanks and legs are more of a gamble that depends on the individual cat. Just as important as where you touch is who starts the interaction and whether you notice when the cat has had enough.

The head and cheeks come out on top

The region between the eye and the ear is called the temporal region. Early work by Susan Soennichsen and A. S. Chamove, published in Anthrozoös in 2002, compared how cats responded to being petted by their owners in different areas. As later researchers summarise it, stimulation of the temporal region tended to bring more positive responses, while the caudal region, around the base of the tail, tended to bring the opposite.

In the introduction to their 2021 study, Haywood, Finka and colleagues sum up the earlier research this way: touching cats' temporal regions "is likely to induce more positive responses," touching the caudal region "may have the opposite effect," and responses to the perioral area, flanks, stomach and back are more varied and depend on the individual cat.

The base of the tail is the area to avoid

A 2015 study in Applied Animal Behaviour Science by Ellis, Thompson, Guijarro and Zulch tested this more formally. In two experiments, cats were stroked in set body regions by an unfamiliar person and by a familiar one, and their behaviour was scored. In both experiments the caudal region produced the highest negative scores: a score of 2.0 against 0.0 to 1.0 for the other regions with the unfamiliar handler, and 2.0 against 1.0 for each other region with the familiar handler. The order in which regions were touched made no significant difference. The authors concluded that handling of cats should avoid the caudal region.

One surprise in that study was that cats showed more negative behaviour when stroked by a familiar person than by an unfamiliar one (scores of 8.5 versus 5.0). The authors did not claim cats dislike their owners. They flagged it as something that needs more research into the owner and cat relationship, and it is a reminder that being familiar with a person does not mean a cat wants to be touched everywhere.

Letting the cat choose works better

Haywood and colleagues turned the research into a simple guide built on the letters C, A and T: give the cat choice and control, pay attention to its body language, and think about where you touch. The touch advice says most friendly cats prefer the base of the ears and the cheeks, some also under the chin, and suggests avoiding the base of the tail and the tummy while being cautious with the back, flank, legs and tail.

They then tested the guide at Battersea Dogs and Cats Home in London. One hundred cats available for rehoming were filmed in short sessions with 120 members of the public, before and after those people received a brief training session on the guidelines. Across 535 observations, cats showed more friendly and positive behaviour when people followed the guidelines, and less human directed aggression and fewer signs of conflict when they had not yet been trained.

Bites during petting are common, and how people react matters

A 2026 study, the "petting diaries," by Kristina O'Hanley, Lee Niel and colleagues asked 66 owners to log three petting sessions with their cat in one day and to answer a survey about their cat's history. Owners reported that most cats enjoyed the logged sessions (82.7 to 90.6 percent), though some showed warning signs. Over the cat's lifetime, 32.2 percent of owners said they had been scratched or bitten during petting.

Owners who said they used verbal corrections such as "No!" after earlier incidents had higher odds of being scratched or bitten, as did owners who said they stopped petting in response. Homes with explorative enrichment such as boxes and tunnels had lower odds. The authors stress that this is an association and that cause and effect is unclear: owners may shape their cats' reactions, or may simply react this way to cats that were already more likely to bite.

Signs a cat has had enough

The Haywood guide lists subtle cues that a cat may be uncomfortable. These include the cat shaking its head, the fur on its back appearing to ripple, nose licking, going still and stopping purring or rubbing, sharply turning its head toward your hand, a brief burst of self grooming, and a tail that twitches or swishes, especially when held low. Pausing and letting the cat decide whether to come back is in keeping with the "choice" part of the guide.

Cat petting FAQ

Where do cats like to be petted the most?

Research on stroking and petting points to the head: the cheeks, the area between the eyes and ears, and the base of the ears, with some cats also enjoying chin scratches.

Where should you not pet a cat?

The base of the tail produced the most negative responses in a 2015 stroking study, and best practice guidelines from 2021 also suggest avoiding the tummy. Back, flanks, legs and tail vary from cat to cat.

Why does my cat bite me when I pet it?

Studies do not give one answer. Touch in less preferred areas, missed warning signs and the cat not having chosen the interaction are all linked to more negative reactions. A 2026 owner study found that about a third of owners had been scratched or bitten during petting at some point.

Do cats like belly rubs?

Some do, but the stomach is one of the areas where responses vary most, and the 2021 Battersea guidelines suggest avoiding it unless your own cat clearly enjoys it.
