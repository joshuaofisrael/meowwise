Cats bring home dead animals because they are hunters by instinct, not because they are hungry. The leading explanations are that your cat is carrying its catch back to a safe place, or acting out the way mother cats bring prey to their kittens. Either way, it is a sign that your cat treats your home as its own safe territory.

Hunting is hard wired

Domestic cats were domesticated more than 10,000 years ago, but veterinarian Dr. Stephanie Liff told Live Science that most have kept their drive to catch prey. Dr. Stephanie Borns-Weil, head of the Tufts Animal Behavior Clinic, says in Tufts Catnip that cats can act on their predatory drive even when they are not hungry, and are sometimes in it just for the chase. So a well fed cat may still hunt.

Theory one: a mother's lesson

Emmanuelle Baudry, an urban ecologist at Paris Saclay University, told Live Science that the main hypothesis is maternal. In the wild, mother cats hunt and bring food back to their kittens, which feeds them and lets them practice handling prey. Our cats may treat us as rather inefficient kittens. Tufts Catnip notes that the behavior is more common in females, which fits this idea of a misplaced instinct to teach.

PetMD describes a related theory: your cat recognizes you as part of its family and may be inclined to share its catch with you.

Theory two: a safe place to eat

Another explanation is that cats bring their prize back to a place where it is safe from other predators. Dr. Liff told Live Science that cats may feel safer eating prey at home, where they have privacy. PetMD adds that your cat may trust you to keep watch over the catch until it is ready to eat or play with it.

Is it a gift or a thank you?

Probably not in the way people imagine. Dr. Borns-Weil says it is unlikely that a cat is paying you back for the care you give it, or showing off. But leaving prey in your personal space is very much an act of trust for a territorial animal. In her words, it is not quite the same as a gift, but trust itself is a gift.

Why it is still worth reducing

Hunting carries risks for your cat and for wildlife. PetMD warns that rodents can carry parasites such as tapeworms or may have eaten rodent poison, and that prey can bite or scratch. A 2013 systematic review in Nature Communications by Loss, Will and Marra estimated that free ranging domestic cats in the United States kill 1.4 to 3.7 billion birds and 6.9 to 20.7 billion mammals each year, with unowned cats causing most of that toll.

What to do when your cat brings prey home

  • Stay calm. PetMD suggests reacting as little as possible, because a toy or treat at that moment could reward the behavior.
  • Dispose of the animal hygienically and wash your hands.
  • Watch your cat for illness such as vomiting, diarrhea, low appetite, drooling, trouble walking, breathing changes or shaking, and call your vet if you see them.
  • If the animal is alive, contain it carefully and contact a local wildlife organization, as PetMD advises.

Cat prey gifts FAQ

Why does my cat bring me dead animals if it is well fed?

Hunting is instinctive and is not driven only by hunger. Vets say cats may hunt even when they are not hungry.

Is my cat trying to teach me to hunt?

That is one leading theory. Mother cats bring prey to kittens, and pet cats may extend that behavior to their human family.

Should I punish my cat for bringing prey?

No. React calmly, remove the animal and focus on prevention, such as indoor time and play with prey like toys.

Can my cat get sick from prey?

Yes. Rodents can carry parasites or may have eaten poison. Watch for signs of illness and call your vet if you are worried.

Do indoor cats do this too?

Indoor cats often carry toys to their owners instead, which PetMD describes as the same instinctive behavior.
