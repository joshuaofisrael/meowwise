Cats purr mainly to keep in touch with cats and people they trust, most often when they are relaxed, but also to ask for things and sometimes when they are stressed, sick or hurt. A purr is a contact signal more than a happiness meter. Research has also changed the story of how cats make the sound: a 2023 study found that a cat's voice box can produce purr frequencies on its own, with no signal from the brain.

When do cats purr?

Dr. Barbara Sherman, who directs the Behavioral Medicine Service at NC State University's veterinary hospital, describes a researcher who fitted throat microphones to free ranging cats. Purring turned up in many situations: when the cat's caretaker was present, when greeting a familiar cat, while rolling or rubbing, and during drowsy sleep, especially somewhere warm and familiar. Mother cats purr while nursing and kittens purr while they feed.

Some situations seem to switch purring off, including fights, mating, hunting, the presence of prey and a cat's first sniff of catnip. In general, Sherman says, purring goes with friendly contact between cats, or between a cat and a person.

Cats also purr when hurt or stressed

Purring is not proof that a cat feels fine. Sherman notes that cats may purr when in pain, and that vets sometimes find a cat purring after it has been hit by a car. That is why our cat myths page lists "a purring cat is always happy" as a myth. Read the whole cat instead: a purring cat that is hunched, hiding, not eating or flinching when touched may be unwell. Cats Protection's list of pain signs is a useful check, and any sudden change in behavior is worth a call to your vet. Our cat behavior guide covers the body language that tells a relaxed purr from a worried one.

The "solicitation purr": how cats ask for breakfast

Many owners know the insistent purr that starts around mealtime. Karen McComb and colleagues at the University of Sussex studied it in a 2009 paper in Current Biology. They recorded purrs from ten cats at home, some made while the cat was actively seeking food and some made in other situations, and played them, at equal loudness, to 50 people.

  • Food seeking purrs sounded more urgent and less pleasant, even to people who had never owned a cat.
  • The difference was a hidden "cry". On top of the purr's very low basic frequency (about 27 Hz), the team found a higher voiced peak, ranging from 220 to 520 Hz with an average of 380 Hz. It was stronger in food seeking purrs and was the feature that best predicted how urgent people rated them.
  • Removing the cry removed the urgency. When the researchers digitally removed only that voiced peak, people judged the same purrs as clearly less urgent.

The authors suggest cats may be tapping into a general mammal sensitivity to the cries of babies. In other words, your cat may have found a sound that is hard to ignore.

How do cats purr? The 2023 voice box surprise

For decades, the textbook answer was that purring needs constant nerve signals. The brain fires rhythmic bursts, typically 20 to 30 times a second, that make the muscles of the larynx (voice box) open and close the airway as the cat breathes in and out. Most other mammal sounds, including a cat's own meow, work differently: air flowing past the vocal folds makes them vibrate by themselves, like a flag in the wind.

In 2023, Christian Herbst, W. Tecumseh Fitch and colleagues at the University of Vienna and partner institutions tested this by studying the larynges of eight domestic cats in a lab setup, with no nerves or muscle activity involved. All eight produced steady vibrations at typical purring rates of about 25 to 30 Hz when air was blown through them. Under the microscope, the team found pads of connective tissue up to 4 mm across embedded in the cats' vocal folds, which they think lets the folds vibrate unusually slowly.

The authors are careful: their results do not fully rule out the muscle based explanation. But they strongly suggest purring uses the same basic air driven mechanism as meows, possibly boosted by muscle activity. So the brain still decides when a cat purrs, but the low rumble itself may come largely from the special build of the vocal folds.

What your cat's purr is telling you

  1. Relaxed body, soft eyes, maybe a slow blink: a contented, social purr.
  2. Purring while following you to the food bowl, often with meows: very likely a food request. You do not have to give in every time; regular meal times help.
  3. Purring with hiding, a hunched posture, no appetite or a change in litter box habits: possibly pain or illness. Call your vet.
  4. Kittens purring while nursing: normal contact with the mother. More on young cats in kitten care.

Cat purring FAQ

Does purring always mean a cat is happy?

No. Cats usually purr in friendly, relaxed contact, but they can also purr when stressed or in pain. Vets sometimes find injured cats purring, so check the rest of the body language.

Why does my cat purr loudly when it wants food?

A 2009 Current Biology study found that food seeking purrs carry a higher, cry like sound (about 220 to 520 Hz) on top of the low purr. People rated these purrs as more urgent, and removing the cry made them sound less urgent.

How do cats physically make the purring sound?

Air moving through the larynx makes the vocal folds vibrate. A 2023 study showed cat larynges can vibrate at purring rates of 25 to 30 Hz with no nerve input, helped by pads of tissue in the vocal folds, although muscle activity may still play a part.

Do kittens purr?

Yes. Kittens purr while nursing, and mother cats purr while nursing them.
