How to introduce two cats safely

By the MeowWise team · Published 16 November 2026 · Last reviewed: 16 November 2026

Go slowly: separate rooms first, then scent swapping, then barriers, then supervised meetings. Cats Protection recommends this staged approach, and there is no fixed timetable; some pairs need weeks or longer.

Why first impressions matter

Bringing home a second cat can be rewarding, but immediate friendship is not a reasonable expectation. Cats differ in their tolerance of unfamiliar animals, and an established resident may see a newcomer as a threat to space, resources and predictability. A new cat may feel equally insecure in unfamiliar surroundings. The goal of an introduction is therefore not to force play. It is to create repeated calm experiences that allow both cats to become comfortable at their own pace. Cats Protection recommends beginning with a separate sanctuary room, then moving through scent exchanges, visual contact and eventually face-to-face meetings. A relaxed coexistence is a legitimate success even if the cats never groom or sleep beside one another. No method guarantees that every pair will become close companions.

Set up a separate sanctuary room

Before the newcomer arrives, prepare a quiet room with its own litter tray, water, food, hiding places, resting spots and scratching options. The new cat should be able to explore that room without being chased or watched by the resident cat. Keep normal routines steady for the existing cat. That means familiar feeding times, resting areas and opportunities for play. Avoid placing food and litter next to one another just because the room is small. Give the new arrival time to eat, use the tray and rest reliably before trying an encounter. A sanctuary is not a punishment or an isolation contest: it is a predictable place where an anxious animal can recover from the disruption of moving. When a cat hides, allow it to emerge voluntarily instead of pulling it out for an introduction.

Begin with scent rather than sight

Cats rely heavily on scent, and the first exchange need not involve the animals seeing each other. Cats Protection suggests gently wiping a cloth over a cat's cheek or forehead, then placing it where the other cat may inspect or ignore it. This preserves choice, which is central to reducing stress. You can repeat the process in both directions without putting a scented cloth directly into a sleeping cat's face. If one cat growls, retreats or becomes unusually watchful, give it more time. A little curiosity is encouraging, but the absence of immediate enthusiasm is not a failure. The point is to make the new smell ordinary. Avoid rushing to an open-door greeting just because the cats can smell each other without hissing on one occasion.

Progress through protected visual meetings

Once both cats respond comfortably to exchanged scent, brief sightlines through a secure barrier may be appropriate. A glass door can offer initial visual contact without physical access. Later, a secure mesh barrier or suitably fitted gate can allow sight and smell together. Check for openings a small kitten could squeeze through. Offer food or play at comfortable distances, not so close that a cat must approach an animal it fears to eat. Allow each cat to walk away. End meetings while they are calm rather than waiting for an argument. Watch for prolonged staring, flattened ears, low crouching, hissing and blocking behaviour. A short episode of tension is a signal to adjust the setup, not an invitation to let the cats 'sort it out' through fighting.

Plan the first shared-room session

Face-to-face contact should happen only after the barrier stages are consistently uneventful. Give both cats escape routes and elevated places to retreat. Keep the first shared time short, supervise it and avoid physically holding them together. A toy can provide a positive distraction if both cats are comfortable playing; it should never be used to lure one cat into a corner. If a confrontation builds, interrupt visual contact safely with a barrier and separate the cats. Do not grab fighting cats with bare hands. Return to the last successful stage for a while rather than concluding the entire process has failed. Some cats settle quickly, while others need weeks or longer. There is no universal calendar for advancing between stages, as Cats Protection notes.

Prevent resource competition after introductions

Living together peacefully requires more than a successful first meeting. Multiple food and water stations, separate resting places, scratching surfaces, litter trays and escape routes reduce the opportunity for guarding. Cats Protection advises having plenty of essentials such as food bowls, litter trays and beds for each cat so they do not feel they have to compete; spreading them around the home rather than clustering them in one spot is a sensible way to do this. This matters because a timid cat can be intimidated without a dramatic fight. Blocking a doorway or sitting between another cat and its litter tray may be enough to cause distress. Watch for reduced appetite, hiding, toileting changes, stalking or one cat consistently controlling access to rooms. If these persist, separate the animals and seek advice from a veterinarian or qualified feline behaviour professional. Medical pain or illness can also change tolerance and should not be mistaken automatically for a personality clash.

Introducing cats FAQ

How long do cat introductions take?

There is no fixed duration; some pairs take weeks or longer.

Can I introduce a kitten directly to an adult?

Follow the same stages, and ensure barriers are kitten-proof.

Is hissing a sign they will never get along?

Not necessarily; it means the cat needs more distance or time.

Should they share one litter box?

Separate resources help prevent conflict.

Do cats need a feline friend?

Not every cat wants one; individual temperament matters.
