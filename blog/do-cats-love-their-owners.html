Do cats love their owners?

By the MeowWise team · Published 11 October 2026 · Last reviewed 11 October 2026

Yes, most cats form a real bond with the people who care for them. In a 2019 study in Current Biology, about two thirds of kittens and adult cats showed a secure attachment to their owner, using the same kind of test used with human babies and dogs. Cats tend to show that bond quietly, and not every study agrees on how much they rely on us, so the honest answer is "yes, in their own way."

The 2019 study: cats show the same attachment styles as babies and dogs

Kristyn Vitale, Alexandra Behnke and Monique Udell at Oregon State University used a "secure base test." A cat spent two minutes in an unfamiliar room with its owner, two minutes alone, and then two minutes with the owner again. Researchers watched the reunion. A securely attached cat greets the returning person and then relaxes and goes back to exploring, as if the person makes the strange room feel safe. An insecurely attached cat stays stressed: it may cling to the owner, keep its distance, or do a confusing mix of both.

  • Kittens (3 to 8 months old): 70 could be classified. Of those, 64.3% were securely attached and 35.7% insecurely attached. Most of the insecure kittens (84%) were the clingy "ambivalent" type.
  • Adult cats (over 1 year): of 38 cats, 65.8% were securely attached and 34.2% insecurely attached, almost the same split as the kittens.
  • Training did not change it: some kittens went through a six week training and socialization class with their owner. Their attachment styles about two months later were no different from kittens who did not take the class.

The university's news release points out that about 65% of human infants are securely attached to their caregiver, so the cat figures land very close to the human ones. The authors argue that attachment to people is not something only dogs do.

The study that disagreed

A 2015 study in PLOS ONE by Alice Potter and Daniel Mills at the University of Lincoln reached a more cautious conclusion. They tested 20 cats and their owners with a carefully counterbalanced version of the classic "strange situation" test, where the owner and a stranger take turns leaving the cat. Cats meowed more when their owner left than when the stranger left, but the team found no other sign that cats used their owner as a source of safety. They concluded that adult cats are usually quite independent, even in their relationships, and that new methods are needed to describe the cat and owner bond.

These results do not have to cancel each other out. The two teams used different tests, different scoring and different numbers of cats. Read together, they suggest many cats are attached to their people, but cats often do not lean on a person for comfort in a stressful place the way a puppy or toddler would.

Cats often choose people over food

A 2017 study in Behavioural Processes from the same Oregon State lab offered pet cats and shelter cats a choice among human social interaction, food, toys and scents. Individual cats varied a lot, but social interaction with a person was the most preferred category for most cats, ahead of food. That held for both pet cats and shelter cats.

Signs your cat is bonded to you

These are common everyday signs of a comfortable, trusting relationship. They are not the scoring rules from the studies above:

  1. Greeting you when you come home, sometimes with a raised tail or a short meow.
  2. Slow blinking at you. Research suggests cats use slow blinks in friendly exchanges with people; see why cats slow blink.
  3. Choosing to be near you, even if not on your lap: sleeping in the same room, following you between rooms.
  4. Reacting to your voice. Cats can tell their owner's voice and their own name apart from others; see do cats know their names?
  5. Relaxing and exploring when you are around in a new place, which is the behavior the secure base test looks for.

How to strengthen the bond

The studies did not test bonding tips, but their findings point in a sensible direction. Offer short, regular play and petting sessions, since many cats value attention as much as treats. Let your cat come to you and stop when it walks away, which keeps contact predictable and safe. Keep routines steady around meals and play. If a friendly cat suddenly becomes clingy, hides or stops greeting you, check with a vet, because pain and illness often show up first as changes in behavior. Cat love and attachment FAQ

Do cats actually love their owners?

Most cats form a genuine bond. In a 2019 Current Biology study, 64.3% of kittens and 65.8% of adult cats showed secure attachment to their owner, close to the roughly 65% figure for human infants.

Are cats less attached than dogs?

The 2019 study found cats show the same main attachment styles as dogs and babies. A 2015 PLOS ONE study found adult cats did not rely on their owner for security in a strange place, so cats may show their bond more independently.

Do cats prefer people or food?

In a 2017 Behavioural Processes study, most pet and shelter cats preferred social interaction with a person over food, toys or scents, though individual cats varied.

Can training make my cat more attached?

In the 2019 study, a six week training and socialization class did not change kittens' attachment styles. Gentle, predictable daily interaction is still good for your relationship.
