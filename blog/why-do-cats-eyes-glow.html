Why do cats' eyes glow in the dark?

By the MeowWise team · Published 28 October 2026 · Last reviewed: 28 October 2026

Cats' eyes glow because of a reflective layer at the back of the eye called the tapetum lucidum. Light that passes through the retina without being absorbed hits this layer and bounces back out through the photoreceptors a second time. That second pass helps a cat see in dim light, and the light that escapes the eye again is the "eyeshine" you see in a torch beam, a car's headlights or a camera flash. The eyes are not making light. They are reflecting it.

What the tapetum lucidum is

Most of the retina's job is to catch photons. In dim light, many photons pass straight through the photoreceptor layer without being absorbed, and in a human eye they are mostly lost in dark pigment behind the retina. Many vertebrates instead have a biological reflector behind (or within) the retina. In a 2004 review in Veterinary Ophthalmology, Franck Ollivier and colleagues describe the tapetum lucidum as a reflector that gives the light sensitive retinal cells "a second opportunity for photon-photoreceptor stimulation," improving visual sensitivity at low light levels. They link the familiar phenomenon of eyeshine directly to this structure.

The tapetum is not built the same way in every animal. Ollivier and colleagues sort it by where it sits and what it is made of:

  • Tapetum cellulosum, a layer of reflective cells in the choroid (the vascular layer behind the retina). This is the type they list for carnivores, which includes cats, as well as rodents and cetaceans.
  • Tapetum fibrosum, made of collagen fibres, found in cows, sheep, goats and horses.
  • Retinal tapetum, inside the retina itself, in teleost fish, crocodilians, marsupials and a fruit bat.
  • Choroidal guanine tapetum, built from guanine crystals, in sharks and rays.

They also note which animals lack one: primates (including us), squirrels, birds, the red kangaroo and the pig, which they describe as usually diurnal. That is why a person's eyes do not shine back at you in a torch beam the way a cat's do.

An old trick that evolved more than once

Ian Schwab and colleagues compared tapeta in fish, crocodiles, a moth family and the cat family in a 2002 paper in Transactions of the American Ophthalmological Society. They concluded that tapeta evolved independently in at least three separate orders of invertebrates and vertebrates, probably as early as the Devonian period, some 345 to 395 million years ago. Different tapeta reflect light in different physical ways, including thin film interference and several kinds of scattering. Their summary is useful for cat owners: each tapetum reflects the wavelengths most relevant to the species' ecological niche. In other words, the reflector is tuned to the light conditions an animal actually hunts or forages in.

The pupil is the other half of night vision

A reflector only helps if the eye can open wide in the dark and still protect itself in bright sun. The cat's vertical slit pupil does exactly that. In Science Advances (2015), Martin Banks and colleagues note that slit pupils close with the help of two extra muscles that compress the opening from the sides. As a result, the domestic cat's pupil can change in area by about 135 fold, compared with roughly 15 fold for the round human pupil. Their analysis of 214 land species found that vertical slit pupils are strongly linked to ambush predators that are active both day and night, which fits the domestic cat well. They also argue that a vertical slit gives sharp depth of field for vertical edges, which may help a crouching hunter judge distance.

Tomas Malmström and Ronald Kröger (2006, Journal of Experimental Biology) add an optical reason. Many animals with good low light vision have multifocal lenses, with rings that focus different colours. A round pupil that constricts would cut off the outer rings, but a slit keeps the full width of the lens in use even in bright light. In their survey, slit pupils turned up only in animals with multifocal lenses. Among felids, small species such as the domestic cat had multifocal lenses and slit pupils, while large species had round pupils and monofocal lenses, a difference the authors call mysterious.

What eyeshine means at home

  1. Glowing eyes are normal. They show that light is reaching the reflective layer and bouncing back toward you. Nothing in the eye is producing light.
  2. Eyeshine is a sign of a dim light specialist. Animals that lack a tapetum, such as people, squirrels and most birds, are mostly active by day.
  3. It is a dim light advantage, not perfect night vision. In very low light a cat's rod dominated vision is mostly about brightness and motion, with little colour (see can cats see color?). No eye sees in total darkness.
  4. Know when to look closer. Normal eyeshine is not a worry, but a pupil that stays a different size from the other one, or an eye that looks cloudy, red, weepy or painful, is a reason to call your vet. Our health page covers other signs worth acting on.

Cat eyeshine FAQ

Why do cats' eyes glow in the dark?

A reflective layer behind the retina, the tapetum lucidum, bounces light back through the photoreceptors. That improves sensitivity in dim light, and the reflected light leaving the eye is what you see as glowing eyes.

Do cats' eyes actually produce light?

No. Eyeshine only appears when a light source, such as a torch, headlights or a flash, shines into the eye. In true darkness there is nothing to reflect.

Why don't human eyes glow like cats' eyes?

Primates, including humans, do not have a tapetum lucidum, according to the 2004 comparative review by Ollivier and colleagues, so there is no reflective layer to send light back out in the same way.

Why do cats have slit pupils?

Slit pupils can change area far more than round ones, about 135 fold in the domestic cat versus about 15 fold in humans, which suits an animal active in both bright day and dim night. Research also links them to ambush hunting and to multifocal lenses.
